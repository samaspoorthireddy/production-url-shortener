You are building SkillSwap — our two-sided marketplace. You have a list of things to build: user profiles, search, booking, payment processing, provider dashboards, reviews, notifications. Some of those items are familiar territory. You have built CRUD forms before. You have built login pages. Those feel safe.\n\nBut payment processing? That means integrating with a third-party API like Stripe. You have never done it. The documentation looks dense. There are webhooks, idempotency keys, test modes, and edge cases around refunds. That feels scary.\n\nThe natural instinct is to start with the comfortable stuff. Knock out the profile page, build the search UI, get some quick wins. You will feel productive. You will have something to show.\n\nBut here is the problem: if payment processing does not work, you do not have a product. SkillSwap without payments is a browsing website. If the profile page has a CSS bug, you still ship. If payments fail, nothing else matters.\n\nThe longer you delay discovering that something does not work, the more you have built on top of a broken assumption. Every day you spend building features that depend on payments working is a day you might have to throw away.\n\nThe Oil Well Analogy\n\nThink of it like drilling for oil. You have a piece of land and a plan to build a pipeline, a refinery, and a distribution network. That is a lot of infrastructure. But before you build any of it, you do one thing first: you drill a test well. You need to know if there is actually oil down there.\n\nIf the test well comes up dry, you have lost the cost of one test well. That hurts, but it is manageable.\n\nIf you build the pipeline first and THEN discover there is no oil, you have lost everything.\n\nBuilding the scariest thing first is drilling the test well. You are testing your most critical assumption before you invest in everything that depends on it.\n\nA Real-World Cautionary Tale\n\nA startup — real company, name withheld — spent three months building a polished frontend for their product. Beautiful UI. Responsive design. Animations. The works. Their core feature depended on pulling data from a third-party API.\n\nThree months in, they finally got around to integrating that API. They discovered the API had a rate limit of 100 requests per hour. Their product needed roughly 10,000 requests per hour to function. The core feature was impossible as designed.\n\nThree months of UI work. All of it built on the assumption that the API would cooperate. They had to redesign the entire product architecture, and most of that beautiful frontend went into the trash.\n\nIf they had spent one afternoon testing the API's limits first — drilling that test well — they would have discovered the problem on day one, not day ninety.\n\nMicro-Exercise: Rank These Three Tasks (2 Minutes)\n\nYou have three tasks for SkillSwap. You know exactly how to build it. There is nothing that could surprise you and invalidate your plan. Risk is not about difficulty — it is about unknowns.\n\nWhat Is a Spike?\n\nBefore we go further, let me introduce a term you will use for the rest of your career: a spike.\n\nA spike is a scouting mission. Before you move the whole army across a bridge, you send one person ahead to check if the bridge can hold the weight. That scout is not building anything permanent. They are gathering information.\n\nIn software, a spike is a short, time-boxed experiment — usually a few hours — where you build a throwaway proof-of-concept to answer a specific question. \"Can we actually connect to Stripe's API and process a test payment?\" You do not build the production payment system. You do not write tests. You do not handle edge cases. You write the minimum code necessary to answer the question.\n\nIf the answer is yes, you throw away the spike code and build it properly. If the answer is no, you have saved yourself days or weeks of wasted effort.\n\nA spike is not wasted time. It is the cheapest way to buy information.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 2: DECIDE\n\nNo artifact creation yet.\n\nThis module has two decisions.\n\nDecision 1: Risk Framework (decisions.module_03.risk_framework)\n\nBefore you can order by risk, you need a way to categorize it. Saying \"this feels risky\" is not enough. You need a vocabulary that makes risk concrete and communicable — something you could put in a document and another engineer would understand exactly what you mean.\n\nHere are four types of risk. Each one represents a different kind of unknown.\n\nIntegration Risk — Will this external thing work with our thing?\n\nThis is the \"will the bridge hold?\" risk. You are connecting your system to something you do not control: a third-party API, a legacy database, a partner's service. The unknown is not whether you can write good code — it is whether the thing on the other end behaves the way you need it to.\n\nEveryday analogy: You are planning a road trip and your route depends on a ferry crossing. You can plan the perfect drive, but if the ferry is not running that day, your plan breaks. The ferry is integration risk.\n\nFor SkillSwap: payment processing through Stripe, sending confirmation emails through an email service, connecting to a maps API for provider location search.\n\nNovelty Risk — Have we ever built something like this before?\n\nThis is the \"uncharted territory\" risk. The technology exists, nobody is stopping you from building it, but neither you nor your team has done anything like it before. You do not know what you do not know.\n\nEveryday analogy: You have cooked pasta a hundred times, but you have never made sourdough bread. The ingredients are available. The recipe exists. But you are going to hit problems you cannot predict because you have never been through this process. That is novelty risk.\n\nFor SkillSwap: real-time availability (making sure two users cannot book the same time slot simultaneously — this is a concurrency problem most junior developers have never solved), the provider vetting workflow (multi-step approval process with state management).\n\nDependency Risk — Does this block everything else if it fails?\n\nThis is the \"single point of failure\" risk. Some components sit at the base of your dependency graph. If they do not work, nothing built on top of them works either. The unknown is not whether they are hard to build — it is how catastrophic the impact is if something goes wrong.\n\nEveryday analogy: The foundation of a house. Pouring concrete is not particularly mysterious work. But if the foundation cracks, you are not fixing a foundation — you are fixing an entire house.\n\nFor SkillSwap: the user authentication system (almost everything requires knowing who the user is), the core data model (providers, services, bookings — everything reads and writes to these).\n\nScale Risk — Will this work when the numbers get big?\n\nThis is the \"what works for 10 users might break for 10,000\" risk. Your design works perfectly in development and testing, but the assumptions embedded in it collapse under real load.\n\nEveryday analogy: A lemonade stand works great at the end of your driveway. But if someone puts you on the news and 500 people show up, everything breaks — you run out of cups, the line wraps around the block, you cannot make lemonade fast enough. The concept still works. The implementation does not scale.\n\nFor SkillSwap: multi-city expansion (the spec says 5 cities in 6 months — is the data model city-aware? Does search assume a single geography?), the \"feel instant\" search requirement (works fine with 50 providers, what about 50,000?).\n\nNow here is the question for you: How do you want to categorize the risks in your dependency graph?\n\nYou could use these four types as-is. You could combine some of them (maybe dependency risk and integration risk feel similar to you). You could add a fifth type if you think something is missing.\n\nThe important thing is that your framework is concrete enough that if you told a teammate \"this has high integration risk,\" they would know exactly what you mean.\n\nProbing questions:\n\n- Can you think of a SkillSwap item that has more than one type of risk? What happens when risks overlap?\n- Which risk type is hardest to estimate? Why?\n- Is there a type of risk you have personally experienced in a past project?\n\nStore the student's framework choice as decisions.module_03.risk_framework.\n\nDecision 2: First Build Target (decisions.module_03.first_build_target)\n\nYou have identified the riskiest item in your build plan. Now: how do you handle it?\n\nOption A: Spike first — scout before you commit.\n\nSpend 2 hours on a throwaway proof-of-concept. For payment processing, that means: create a Stripe test account, write the minimum code to charge a test card, and see what comes back. No error handling. No UI. No tests. Just: \"Can we do this?\"\n\nThe upside: if the spike reveals a problem, you have lost 2 hours, not 2 weeks. You can redesign before you have committed to an approach.\n\nThe downside: the spike code gets thrown away. Those 2 hours produced no production code. Some people see this as \"waste.\" You also might learn that the spike works fine but the real implementation is harder — the spike gives you confidence that might be false.\n\nOption B: Build it for real first — make the riskiest item the first production ticket.\n\nSkip the spike. Make your first real ticket \"Implement payment processing.\" Build it properly: error handling, tests, edge cases, production-ready.\n\nThe upside: no wasted work. Everything you build is real. If it works, you are ahead of schedule.\n\nThe downside: if the approach does not work, you have invested significantly more before discovering the problem. You have production code that needs to be rewritten, not throwaway code that was always meant to be deleted. Psychologically, throwing away \"real\" code is much harder than throwing away a spike.\n\nProbing:\n\n- If the spike reveals the API will not work, what did the spike cost you versus what did it save you?\n- When might Option B be the better choice? (Think about cases where the risk is moderate and the riskiest thing is also the most well-understood path.)\n- Is there a middle ground — a spike that becomes the foundation of the real implementation?\n\nThink about the startup from our earlier story. If they had spent one afternoon on a spike — just hitting the API and checking the rate limits — they would have saved three months. The spike would have cost them 4 hours and saved them 90 days.\n\nBut consider a different scenario: if the riskiest item is your database schema design, a spike might not help. You cannot \"test\" a schema in 2 hours — the risk only materializes when you try to build real features on top of it. In that case, building it for real and learning from the experience might be the better path.\n\nStore the student's choice as decisions.module_03.first_build_target.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 3: ARTIFACT CREATION\n\nArtifact creation starts now.\n\nWrite this module's risk annotations and ordered list in\nartifacts/risk-plan-module-03.md.\n\nNow it is time to apply this thinking to your actual SkillSwap dependency graph.\n\nPart 1: Annotate Every Node (10 Minutes)\n\nGo back to the dependency graph you built in Module 2. Sometimes the riskiest item depends on something else being built first. Payment processing is high-risk, but it probably depends on having a user model and basic authentication. You cannot just jump straight to payments on day one.\n\nThe student's job is to find the best ordering that gets to high-risk items as fast as possible while still respecting the dependency chain.\n\nExample format for the build order:\n\n1. User data model + auth (Risk: 1, Dependency) — Low risk but required by everything. Build this first because it unblocks the most work.\n2. Lots of fields is tedious, not risky.\n- Dependency respect: The order must not violate hard dependencies. If payment processing depends on auth, auth must come first.\n- Justifications: Each position should have a reason. \"I put this here because it felt right\" is not sufficient. \"I put this here because it has the highest integration risk and everything from item 6 onward depends on it working\" is what we want.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 4: VERIFY\n\nAsk the student to walk through their plan:\n\n\"Walk me through what you would learn in the first 3 items. After building items 1, 2, and 3 — what do you know that you did not know before? What risks have you retired?\"\n\nExpected: The first few items should retire the biggest unknowns. What have you wasted?\"\n\nThis question tests whether the student understands the value of early failure. If item 1 fails on day 2, you have lost 1 day. If item 1 fails on day 30 because you saved it for last, you have potentially lost 30 days of work built on a false assumption.\n\nNow the targeted push:\n\n\"You put auth as item 2. What is the risk in auth? Is it genuinely risky, or is it just foundational?\"\n\nThis is a critical distinction. Authentication is low-risk — it is a well-solved problem with mature libraries. But it is high-dependency — many other items need it to exist. Being foundational is not the same thing as being risky.\n\nIf the student conflated \"lots of things depend on this\" with \"this is risky,\" help them see the difference:\n\n- Risk asks: \"How likely is this to fail or surprise me?\"\n- Dependency asks: \"How many other things are blocked until this is done?\"\n\nAuth is high-dependency, low-risk. Payment processing is (potentially) lower-dependency but much higher-risk. In a risk-first ordering, you build auth early because it unblocks risky items, not because auth itself is risky.\n\nThis is one of the most common mistakes in risk ordering: confusing critical-path items with risky items. A mature engineer builds foundational items early to unblock the risky items, and then tackles risk head-on.\n\nAdditional verification questions:\n\n- \"Is there any item in your bottom 3 that has a hidden risk you might be underestimating?\"\n- \"If you could only build 5 of these items before a demo, which 5 would you pick? You cannot solve this with better code, a different library, or a clever architecture. The Stripe API could be perfectly easy to integrate — it does not matter if there is no Stripe account to connect it to.\n\nThis is a business blocker. It lives outside the codebase entirely.\n\nAsk the student:\n\n- \"What type of risk is this? Does it fit into the four categories we defined, or is it something different?\"\n- \"Your risk-ordered plan has payment processing as one of the top 3 items. The four risk types we defined (integration, novelty, dependency, scale) are all technical risks — things that could go wrong in the code or architecture. Guide them through the thought process:\n\nStep 1: Isolate the Blocked Work\n\nPayment processing is blocked, but not everything that seems related to payments is actually blocked. Help the student separate:\n\n- Truly blocked: Actual money movement, Stripe integration, commission calculation on real transactions, refund processing\n- Not blocked: The interface for payments (what the booking system expects to send and receive), the data model for transactions, the UI for displaying pricing\n\nStep 2: Define a Stubbed Interface\n\nThe student should design a payment interface — a clear contract that says \"when the rest of the system needs to process a payment, it calls this function with these inputs and expects this output.\" Behind that interface, the implementation is a stub: it always returns \"payment successful\" in development, or it logs the request and moves on.\n\nThe contract might look something like:\n\nprocessPayment(booking_id, amount, user_payment_method) → { status: success | failed, transaction_id }\n\nThe key insight: when the business blocker is resolved and a Stripe account exists, the team swaps the stub for a real implementation. Everything that depends on payments continues to work because it talks to the interface, not to Stripe directly.\n\nThis is a pattern the student will use for their entire career: when something is blocked, define the contract and stub the implementation. Keep building.\n\nStep 3: Escalate to the PM\n\nThe student should recognize that this needs to be communicated immediately — not just noted in the plan. We are stubbing the payment interface so development continues, but real payment testing is blocked until the client resolves this. Can you get us a timeline?\"\n\nBad example: \"Payments are hard, we will do them later.\" (This hides the real problem and does not escalate.)\n\nStep 4: Plan for the Rest of the Team\n\n\"What does the rest of the team work on while payments are blocked?\"\n\nThis question tests whether the student can think about team flow, not just their own work. The answer should reference their dependency graph and risk ordering:\n\n- The booking system can be built against the stubbed payment interface\n- Provider dashboards, search, reviews — none of these are blocked by payments\n- The items that were already lower in the risk-ordered plan become the team's focus\n- When the business blocker resolves, payment integration moves back to the top of the queue\n\nIf the student says \"we just wait,\" push back hard. A blocked item should never stop an entire team. That is the whole point of dependency mapping and interface contracts — they let work continue around blockers.\n\nThe Updated Plan\n\nThe student should produce a revised build order that:\n\n1. Replaces \"Payment processing\" with \"Payment interface contract + stub\"\n2. Adds \"Payment integration (blocked — waiting on client Stripe account)\" as a future item\n3. Notes the escalation to PM\n4. Shows that other high-risk items (real-time availability, multi-city data model) move up in priority since the team has bandwidth\n\n---\n\nSTEP 7: REFLECT\n\nGuided Reflection\n\nAsk the student these questions.
