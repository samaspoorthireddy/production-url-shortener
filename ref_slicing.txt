There are two ways to serve it.\n\nWay 1: Layer by layer. You give everyone the bottom layer of cake first. Then, once everyone has that, you give them the cream. Then, once everyone has the cream, you give them the frosting. Nobody tastes the actual cake — the combination — until every layer is done. If the cream is bad, you do not find out until sprint three.\n\nWay 2: Slice by slice. You cut a thin wedge. It is narrow — just one slice — but it has a bit of every layer. Cake, cream, frosting. One bite tells you if the combination works.\n\nThat is the difference between horizontal and vertical slicing.\n\nHorizontal slicing is building by layer: all the database models first, then all the API endpoints, then all the UI. It feels productive because each layer is \"complete.\" But nobody can use the system until every layer is done. And if the layers do not fit together, you find out last.\n\nVertical slicing is building by feature thread: a thin wedge that goes from database to API to UI for ONE specific user action. It is narrow — it does not cover every feature. But it is end-to-end. A real person can use it. You learn something immediately.\n\nThe Team That Built the Data Layer for Three Sprints\n\nReal story. A team of five engineers was building an internal tool for operations. The tech lead decided to \"get the foundation right\" — they spent three sprints (six weeks) building every database model, every migration, every seed file. The data layer was thorough, well-tested, beautifully normalized.\n\nAt the sprint review after those six weeks, the product manager asked: \"Can I see it?\"\n\nThere was nothing to see. No UI. No API. No way for a human to interact with the system. Six weeks of work and the only evidence was a database schema diagram.\n\nWorse: when they finally built the first API endpoint, they discovered the data model did not match the actual workflow. The operations team needed to handle partial bookings — something the schema did not support because nobody had tested an end-to-end flow. Three weeks of rework on the \"solid foundation.\"\n\nIf they had built one thin vertical slice — one operation, end to end, ugly UI and all — they would have found the partial booking problem in week one, not week nine.\n\nMicro-Exercise (3 Minutes)\n\nTake this requirement from the SkillSwap spec: \"A user can book a service provider.\"\n\nDraw two things (on paper, in a text editor, however you think):\n\n1. The horizontal layers. What are the distinct layers this feature touches? (Hint: there are at least three — data storage, backend logic, user interface.)\n\n2. The minimal vertical slice. What is the absolute thinnest version of this feature that goes end to end? Not the full booking system — the thinnest wedge that lets a human complete a booking action and see a result.\n\nThink about it this way: what is the ONE provider, the ONE service, the ONE time slot, and the ONE booking that proves the concept? Everything else is a wider slice for later.\n\nWrite it down before continuing. The horizontal layers should be obvious. The vertical slice is the interesting part — and what you include versus exclude defines your skill at scoping.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 2: DECIDE\n\nNo artifact creation yet.\n\nTwo decisions in this step. Both shape the rest of your build.\n\nDecision 1: What Goes in the First Slice (decisions.module_04.slice_boundary)\n\nYou are building SkillSwap — a two-sided marketplace. There are two sides. The first slice can start from either one.\n\nOption A: Supply side first — Provider registration and listing.\n\nBuild the provider experience: a provider signs up, creates a profile, lists a service, sets availability. At the end of this slice, you have real providers in the system. But there is nothing for users to do — no browsing, no booking. You are testing half the marketplace.\n\nUpside: You have real data from real providers. When you build the demand side next, it connects to something real, not mocks.\n\nDownside: You cannot demonstrate the core value proposition. A stakeholder looking at this slice sees a registration form, not a marketplace. The question \"does anyone actually want to book through this?\" remains completely unanswered.\n\nOption B: Demand side first — Browse and book with hardcoded providers.\n\nBuild the user experience: a user sees a list of providers (seeded data, not real providers), picks one, selects a time slot, completes a booking, sees a confirmation. At the end of this slice, a human can walk through the core flow. But the providers are fake — hardcoded seed data.\n\nUpside: A stakeholder can click through it. The core value proposition is testable. You learn whether the booking flow makes sense before building the supply side.\n\nDownside: Provider data is fake. You have not proven that real providers can or will use the system. The supply side is entirely untested.\n\nProbing questions to consider:\n\n- Which slice teaches you more about whether the product works? Not whether the code works — whether the product works.\n- Which one can a stakeholder click through and give you feedback on?\n- If you only had time to build ONE slice before a go/no-go decision, which one answers the question that matters most?\n\nThere is a defensible case for either option. But the reasoning matters more than the choice. \"I just think demand side is more important\" is not a justification. \"The biggest product risk is whether users will complete the booking flow, and only Option B tests that\" is.\n\nStore your decision as decisions.module_04.slice_boundary = supply_first | demand_first.\n\nDecision 2: How Thin Is Thin Enough (decisions.module_04.mvp_scope)\n\nYou have picked a side. Now: how thin is the slice?\n\nThis is the scoping discipline that separates experienced engineers from everyone else. The instinct is always to add more. \"While we are at it, we should add search.\" \"It would be weird without auth.\" \"Just a basic payment flow.\"\n\nEach of those additions sounds small. Each one doubles the build time or introduces a new integration risk.\n\nThe question is: what is the absolute minimum that lets a human test the core hypothesis?\n\nFor a demand-side-first slice, that might be:\n\n- 3 hardcoded providers (not a dynamic list, not a search, just 3 cards)\n- Pick one provider, see their single service\n- Pick a time slot from a hardcoded list (not a real calendar, not real availability)\n- Click \"book\" and see a confirmation screen\n- No payment. No auth. No email. No cancellation. No review.\n\nThat is thin. Uncomfortably thin. And that is the point.\n\nIf you are not slightly uncomfortable with how little is in the slice, you have not cut thin enough.\n\nProbing questions:\n\n- Can you remove one more thing from your scope and still have something testable?\n- If you add auth, what does that teach you that the slice without auth does not?\n- What is the estimated build time? If it is more than 2 hours, what can you cut?\n\nStore your decision as decisions.module_04.mvp_scope = ultra_thin | moderate | broad with a justification.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 3: ARTIFACT CREATION\n\nArtifact creation starts now.\n\nWrite your 4-5 slice definitions in artifacts/slices-module-04.md.\n\nDefine Your Vertical Slices (15–20 Minutes)\n\nNow produce 4–5 vertical slice definitions for SkillSwap. These slices should collectively cover the path from \"nothing exists\" to \"core marketplace works end to end.\" They should be ordered — each slice builds on the previous one.\n\nFor each slice, define:\n\n1. Name — A short, descriptive name. \"Browse and Book (Hardcoded)\" is good. \"Slice 1\" is not.\n\n2. Scope (What Is In) — Every feature, behavior, and artifact this slice delivers. Be specific. Not \"booking works\" but \"user selects a provider from a list of 3, picks a time slot, clicks Book, sees a confirmation page with booking ID and provider name.\"\n\n3. Anti-Scope (What Is Explicitly Out) — This is the most important field. Anti-scope is not \"everything else.\" It is a specific list of things someone might reasonably expect to be included but are NOT. This prevents scope creep during build and prevents misunderstandings during review.\n\nBad anti-scope: \"Other features.\"\nBad anti-scope: \"Stuff we will do later.\"\nGood anti-scope: \"No user authentication — all actions are anonymous. No payment processing — booking is free. No email confirmation — confirmation is on-screen only. No search or filtering — providers are displayed as a static list. No provider-side interface — provider data is seeded directly into the database. No cancellation flow. No reviews.\"\n\nThe anti-scope should make a stakeholder nod and say \"okay, I understand what I am NOT getting.\" It prevents the conversation where someone sees the demo and says \"but where is the payment?\" You already told them. It is in the anti-scope.\n\n4. Dependencies — Which earlier slices must be complete before this one starts? Slice 1 has no dependencies. Slice 3 might depend on Slices 1 and 2.\n\n5. Acceptance Criteria — How does a non-technical person verify this slice works? Write it as a series of steps a human would take. \"Open the app. See 3 providers listed. Click on 'Maria's Cleaning.' See one service with 3 available time slots. Pick the 2pm slot. Click Book. See confirmation page showing booking ID, provider name, service, and time.\" If the acceptance criteria require reading code or checking a database, they are not acceptance criteria — they are technical verification steps.\n\n6. Estimated Complexity — S (small, a few hours), M (medium, a day or two), L (large, multiple days). The first slice should be S or small M. If your first slice is L, it is not a slice — it is a phase.\n\nExample First Slice (for reference, not to copy)\n\nHere is what a strong first slice looks like for a demand-side-first approach:\n\nName: Browse and Book (Seeded Data)\n\nScope: User visits the app and sees a list of 3 service providers with name, category, and rating. User clicks a provider and sees their profile with one service listed and 3 available time slots. User selects a time slot and clicks \"Book.\" User sees a confirmation screen with booking ID, provider name, service name, and booked time. Booking is persisted to the database.\n\nAnti-scope: No user authentication (anonymous usage). No payment processing (booking is free). No email or push notifications. No search or filtering. No provider registration or management interface. No cancellation or modification of bookings. No reviews or ratings submission. No real-time availability checking (time slots are static seed data). No multi-city support. No admin dashboard.\n\nDependencies: None (first slice).\n\nAcceptance criteria:\n1. Open the app — see 3 provider cards with name, category, star rating.\n2. Click any provider — see profile page with service description and 3 time slots.\n3. Select a time slot and click \"Book Now.\"\n4. See confirmation page with booking reference number, provider name, service, and time.\n5. Refresh the page — booking persists (it is stored, not just in-memory).\n\nEstimated complexity: S (2–3 hours)\n\nNotice the anti-scope list. It is long. It is specific. Every item on it is something a stakeholder might reasonably ask about. By listing them explicitly, you are having the scope conversation BEFORE the build, not during the demo.\n\nYour Turn\n\nDefine 4–5 slices. The slices should progress logically:\n\n- Slice 1: The thinnest possible end-to-end flow (your MVP scope decision from Step 2).\n- Slice 2: Adds the next most valuable capability (authentication? Provider self-service? Search?).\n- Slice 3: Adds the next layer (probably the other side of the marketplace).\n- Slice 4: Addresses a key risk or integration (payment? real-time availability?).\n- Slice 5 (optional): Rounds out the core product (reviews? notifications? admin?).\n\nEach subsequent slice should be slightly larger than the one before, because you have more infrastructure to build on. But none should be an L unless you can justify why it cannot be split further.\n\nPush yourself on anti-scope. For every item in your scope, ask: \"What is the adjacent thing someone might expect that I am NOT building?\" Put that in anti-scope.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 4: VERIFY\n\nThe Clickthrough Test (5 Minutes)\n\nTake your first slice and answer this question:\n\n\"Can I test your first slice without reading any code?\"\n\nWalk through it. Literally narrate the user experience step by step. \"I open the browser. I see... I click... I see...\" If at any point the walkthrough requires technical knowledge (\"then you check the database\" or \"then the API returns a 200\"), the slice is not end-to-end. End-to-end means a human can verify it through the interface.\n\nThen answer:\n\n\"What is the ONE thing you learn from this slice that you did not know before building it?\"\n\nThis is the core question of vertical slicing. Every slice should teach you something. The first slice might teach you: \"The booking flow makes sense to users\" or \"Three providers is enough to feel like a marketplace, or it is not\" or \"The time slot selection UX needs work.\"\n\nIf the answer is \"I learn that the database works\" — that is not a product learning. That is a technical verification. A vertical slice produces product insight, not just technical confirmation.\n\nRed Flags to Check\n\nReview your slice definitions for these problems:\n\nToo thick: The slice includes authentication AND payment AND email notifications. That is not a slice — that is three slices stacked on top of each other. If it takes more than a day to build, it is too thick. Split it.\n\nToo thin: The slice is just a health endpoint, or just a database migration, or just a static HTML page with no backend. That is a horizontal layer disguised as a slice. A vertical slice has behavior — a user does something and something happens.\n\nVague anti-scope: \"Other features not mentioned.\" This protects you from nothing. When the stakeholder asks \"where is search?\" you need to be able to point to the anti-scope and say \"it is explicitly listed as out of this slice — see item 4.\"\n\nNo acceptance criteria a human can test: \"API returns correct JSON\" is a technical test, not an acceptance criterion. \"User sees their booking confirmation with the correct time\" is something any human can verify.\n\nSlices that do not build on each other: If Slice 3 has no dependency on Slices 1 or 2, either the slices are independent (which is fine but unusual) or you have not thought about what state and infrastructure carries forward.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 5: BREAK\n\nThe Stakeholder Pressure Test\n\nYou have your slice plan. The first slice is clean, thin, well-scoped. You are ready to build.\n\nThen this happens:\n\nFrom the PM (Friday, 4:47 PM):\n\"Hey — great plan. Love the slice approach. Quick thing: I just got off a call with our lead investor. They want to see a demo next Friday. They specifically asked about payments. They want to see money flow through the platform. The booking-without-payment demo would be 'concerning' to them — their words. Can we make sure the first demo includes real payments? Even just test mode is fine. Thanks!\"\n\nYour first slice does not include payments. That was an explicit anti-scope item. You scoped it out deliberately because payment integration is an integration risk you identified in Module 3.\n\nNow you have a decision to make. There are several possible responses:\n\nResponse A: Expand the first slice. Add Stripe test-mode payments to Slice 1. This makes the demo investor-friendly. But it roughly doubles the build time (payment integration, webhook handling, confirmation flow changes), and you risk not finishing by Friday. You also lose the clean learning from the thin first slice — now you are testing the booking flow AND the payment flow at the same time. If something breaks, you do not know which part failed.\n\nResponse B: Add a payment spike slice. Keep Slice 1 as-is. Add a new Slice 1.5: a minimal payment spike that wires Stripe test mode into a standalone page. It is not integrated into the booking flow — it just proves money can flow through the platform. Demo Slice 1 (the booking flow) and Slice 1.5 (the payment proof) side by side. Two thin demos, not one thick one.\n\nResponse C: Push back with reasoning. Go back to the PM with data. \"Adding payments to the first slice doubles the build time and introduces the integration risk we flagged in our risk assessment. Here is what I recommend instead: we demo the booking flow end-to-end and show the payment integration plan with a timeline. The investor sees a working product AND a credible path to payments. If payments are truly a hard requirement for the demo, we need to cut something else — here is what I would cut and what the impact would be.\"\n\nResponse D: Something else. Maybe you have a better idea.\n\nThere is no single correct answer. But there is a wrong one: \"Sure, I will just add payments to Slice 1\" without acknowledging what that costs. That is not a decision — that is capitulation. It is the response of someone who does not understand that saying yes to scope means saying no to something else (finish date, quality, other features, sleep).\n\nThink through your response. What do you recommend, and why? What are you trading away?\n\n---\n\nSTEP 6: FIX\n\nAdjust Your Slice Plan\n\nBased on your response to the stakeholder pressure, update your slice plan.\n\nIf you chose to add a payment spike, show it. Where does it fit? What is in its scope? What is in its anti-scope? How long does it take?\n\nIf you chose to expand Slice 1, show the updated scope AND the updated anti-scope AND the updated time estimate. What did you cut to make room? If you did not cut anything, explain how you absorb the additional scope without the build taking longer. It should include: what you recommend, why, what the alternatives are, and what you need from them to proceed.\n\nWhat we are looking for:\n\n- Explicit reasoning. Not \"I think this is fine\" but \"I chose X because Y, and the tradeoff is Z.\"\n- Scope awareness. If you added something, you acknowledged what it costs. If you cut something, you explained what is lost.\n- Professionalism. Whether you accommodated the request or pushed back, you did it with data and options, not with attitude or blind compliance.\n\nWhat we reject:\n\n- \"We will just add payments to Slice 1\" with no acknowledgment of the scope increase.\n- \"I will work over the weekend to get it done\" — scope management is not about personal sacrifice, it is about making the right tradeoffs visible.\n- \"The investor is wrong, payments do not matter\" — you do not get to dismiss stakeholder concerns. You get to propose alternatives.\n\nAny strategy that is explicitly reasoned and acknowledges tradeoffs is acceptable. The skill is not picking the \"right\" answer — it is making a defensible decision under pressure and communicating it clearly.\n\n---\n\nSTEP 7: REFLECT\n\nWhat You Built This Module\n\nYou took a risk-ordered build plan and transformed it into something actionable: a sequence of vertical slices, each one end-to-end testable, each one building on the last.
